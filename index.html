01

Preserve the strategy that was actually good.

The annual system made sense: create regional familiarity, keep the story fresh, use paid distribution to learn what audiences respond, and connect that learning to priority actions. Good Remedy did not throw away the commercial insight just because the artifact needed work.

02

Separate footprint from promised delivery.

The proposal cited a large network reach. Good Remedy separated the network’s potential scale from what the client could actually expect to receive under a final media schedule. A true fact was prevented from becoming a stronger promise than the record supported.

03

Narrow the outcome claim.

A direct growth outcome was too strong for what the campaign itself could establish. The promise was reframed around awareness, engagement, audience development, inquiry support, and measurable response — still commercially useful, but supportable.

04

Expose the economics that still needed an owner.

A lower-priced fallback package looked credible on the page but still required confirmation that the production scope, media value, third-party costs, and margin could actually be delivered together. Better copy could not resolve that decision.

05

Connect marketing activity to accountable follow-through.

The revised proposal made the handoff visible: landing pages, tracking, analytics, CRM visibility, response handling, and the client’s own follow-up. The campaign could create demand and signals; the client still owned the conversion process.

06

Turn the close into a decision, not a slogan.

The final proposal no longer ended with a broad recommendation alone. It identified the operating items that had to be confirmed before final send: scope, schedule, measurement method, ownership, payment and approval terms, and the economics of the fallback.

What changed:The work became easier to defend because support, limits, ownership, and open decisions traveled with the proposal instead of remaining scattered behind it.
