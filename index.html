The Project Looked Finished. It Wasn't.

What building Good Remedy taught me about false completion in AI-assisted work

Benjamin Lopez · Founder, Good Remedy

At some point, Good Remedy became very good at explaining why it was not yet proven.

We had plans for the proof, records of the proof, reviews of the records, dashboards, registers, and increasingly precise descriptions of what the test should measure. An internal assessment counted roughly 70 artifacts. The exact count mattered less than the pattern: we had built an elaborate system around the proof while the events that could actually complete it were still unresolved.

We still needed human review. We still needed someone outside the project to use the work. We still needed buyer contact, paid use, and a completed proof event.

The embarrassing part was not that the assessment was unfair.

It was that it was right.

I started Good Remedy to pressure-test consequential work against the reliance it asks people to place in it. Then I discovered that Good Remedy's own project could produce increasingly convincing signs of completion without completing the event that mattered.

That is what I mean by false completion.

False completion is what happens when work acquires the appearance or status of being finished before the condition that actually matters has been met.

AI makes this unusually hard to see because it can produce nearly everything we associate with progress. It can write the plan, revise it, summarize the meeting, build the tracker, draft the outreach, diagnose the bottleneck, and turn the whole history into a polished account of what supposedly happened.

Some of that work may be excellent.

The problem begins when it starts standing in for something else.

Activity is not movement

Once I saw the problem in the proof effort, I recognized it in another part of Good Remedy.

We were building a broad commercial outreach operation. There was no shortage of visible activity: company searches, prospect records, public contact routes, scheduled automations, and attempted sends. Every one of those things belonged somewhere in a real sales process.

But they were not the same state.

A company discovered is not a named buyer.

A named buyer is not a verified mailbox.

A public email listing is not proof of deliverability.

A send attempt is not delivery.

Delivery is not a reply.

A reply is not a meeting.

A meeting is not revenue.

Written out, the distinctions seem almost embarrassingly obvious. Inside a large AI-assisted project, they were much easier to blur.

The searches were real. The research was useful. The schedules existed. The attempted sends mattered. The error was not doing adjacent work. The error was allowing legitimate preparation to inherit the status of the result it was meant to produce.

Activity had begun to look like movement.

That matters because AI can generate so much legitimate adjacent work, so quickly, that the volume itself becomes persuasive. A project may be busy, detailed, and increasingly sophisticated while the real bottleneck remains exactly where it was.

A polished sentence can outrun the facts

The same failure can happen inside a single piece of writing.

Consider a synthetic example: a fictional non-medical home-care agency asks AI to draft a landing page. The draft says the agency is available 24/7, suggests that services may begin the same day, refers to participation in certain payer programs, describes support that could sound clinical, and invites families to submit an inquiry form.

All of those statements arrive in the same confident voice. They have not earned the same confidence.

“Available 24/7” might mean that someone answers the telephone, that scheduling staff are reachable, or that caregivers are actually available to begin service at any hour. A same-day start depends on assessment, staffing, location, and the support requested. Payer participation requires a current record. Language about medication, dementia, or help after a hospital stay needs a clear non-clinical boundary. An inquiry form raises a separate question about what information is collected, who sees it, and what happens to it.

AI can smooth those distinctions away. Better language can remove or qualify unsupported claims. It cannot create staffing capacity, payer status, privacy approval, or permission to publish.

The quality of the sentence does not determine the authority of the sentence.

When the proof system became the project

The most unsettling version was still the one inside Good Remedy.

Our proof system was not empty bureaucracy. Much of it was careful and useful. Each review could identify something the previous phase had missed. The project became more honest about its limits and more exact about what still needed to happen.

But the next thing that actually mattered kept turning into another internal artifact.

We had built a system that could tell us, with increasing accuracy, why the proof was not complete. For a while, our response to discovering that was to improve the system that told us so.

That was the turn.

The system meant to support the project had become the project.

The lesson is not that documentation is bad. Consequential work needs records. People need to know what happened, what changed, what remains open, and who is responsible for the next decision.

The failure comes when maintaining that apparatus becomes a substitute for the event it exists to serve.

AI is making finished-looking work cheap and abundant. The plan is easy to produce. So are the summary, the deck, the policy, the tracker, the recommendation, and the polished final answer.

The scarce thing is no longer the artifact.

It is knowing what another person can responsibly rely on.

Someone still has to decide whether the work can be sent, published, implemented, inherited, purchased, or acted upon. That person needs to know what supports it, what remains unresolved, who has reviewed it, who owns the decision, and what can responsibly happen next.

That is the problem Good Remedy exists to address.

The central idea is simpler:

The artifact is not the event.

The description is not the evidence.

Fluency is not authority.

And a project is not finished merely because it has become very good at explaining itself.

The two Good Remedy examples are compressed from internal records, and the home-care example is synthetic. They illustrate the pattern; they do not show how common it is or prove that Good Remedy reliably prevents it.
